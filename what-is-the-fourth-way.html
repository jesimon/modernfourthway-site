What Is the Fourth Way?
The Four Paths — and the One Truth Behind Them

If the diagnosis of Chapter 1 is even half right — if we’re absent from most of our lives, and we can’t simply decide to stay present — then an obvious question follows. Has anyone ever solved this? And if so, HOW?

The answer is yes, and humanity has known three solutions for thousands of years. Gurdjieff’s contribution was a fourth. Not a fourth religion or a fourth philosophy, but a fourth method, engineered for a kind of person the first three were never designed for: the person who cannot, or should not, leave their life.

To see why anyone needed a fourth way, you first have to understand the original three. Not as history — as living strategies you can watch operating in the world right now.

The Three Traditional Ways

Every traditional path to transformation, Gurdjieff taught, works by concentrating all of a person’s force on one part of their nature until something crystallizes. The three ways differ in which part.

One clarification before we look at them, because it prevents a real confusion: these three ways are not religions. They are METHODS — specialized, full-time paths of transformation that appear inside many religions and outside them too. Christianity contains monasteries and also hundreds of millions of faithful of whom renunciation was never asked; and most traditions are not renunciate at all — Judaism above all, whose entire architecture is built for life in the household, the marketplace, and the community, as this chapter will show. The three ways describe what the rare specialist does. Keep that distinction in hand, because the Fourth Way is about to claim the specialist’s goal for the person who stays.

The Way of the Fakir: The Body

The first way works through the physical body: through endurance, austerity, and the deliberate use of suffering to forge will. The traditional image is the Indian fakir holding his arms above his head for years, or standing on one leg for a decade. The modern world has its own fakirs, and you’ve seen them. The ultramarathoner running through mile one hundred on destroyed feet. The ice-bath devotee sitting in frozen water at dawn — not for health, exactly, but for the daily proof of mind over flesh. The mountaineer going back to the death zone again and again. Strip away the branding, and the substance is ancient: mastery of the machine through voluntary suffering.

And it works — narrowly. The fakir develops something real: a will hard enough to override the body’s loudest demands. But Gurdjieff’s assessment was blunt. The fakir develops will and nothing else. Emotion remains childish. Understanding remains untouched. You may have met this person: extraordinary discipline in one channel, and outside that channel, surprisingly mechanical — rigid, easily fooled, emotionally unfinished. The smith forged the instrument, but nobody ever taught him what the instrument was FOR. Years of work, and only one-third of a human being.

The Way of the Monk: The Heart

The second way works through emotion: through faith, devotion, surrender, and love concentrated on the sacred until the emotional nature unifies around it. This is the way of the monastery in every tradition: the Christian monk keeping vigil, the Sufi in remembrance, the devotee whose entire inner life becomes a single flame of devotion. You can see its modern forms too, wherever you find lives organized wholly around devotion and self-giving — and, in diluted form, in the seeker who moves from retreat to retreat chasing the felt state of surrender.

The way of the monk is faster than the way of the fakir, Gurdjieff said, because emotion is a finer and more powerful energy than physical will. And its fruit is real: a unified heart, capable of a faith that most of us touch only in moments. But the same one-sidedness applies. The monk’s body and mind remain undeveloped servants of the flame. Depth of feeling, purity of devotion — and often no trained instrument for understanding WHAT the devotion has found, or a body that’s been treated as an enemy for decades.

The Way of the Yogi: The Mind

The third way works through the mind: through concentration, study of consciousness, and mental discipline pursued until the practitioner gains command over states of awareness itself. The traditional yogi of this description is less the postures-and-breathing figure of the modern studio and more the meditator mapping states of consciousness with scientific patience. Today, think of the serious retreatant with ten thousand hours on the cushion, or the consciousness researcher experimenting on the instrument of their own mind.

This is the fastest of the three, said Gurdjieff, because it works with the finest energy. The accomplished yogi KNOWS — he has looked directly into the mechanics of mind that others only theorize about. But the pattern completes itself: knowledge without a developed heart to give it warmth, or a developed body to give it ground. You may have met this person too. Luminous insight on the cushion, and an ordinary, reactive, sometimes hollow life off of it. The tradition’s own literature is full of warnings about exactly this figure.

What All Three Share

Now look at what all three ways have in common, because the Fourth Way defines itself against it.

First, each demands renunciation as its entry fee. Fakir, monk, and yogi all begin by leaving — the household, the profession, the family, the world. The work happens in a sealed environment: the ashram, the monastery, the cave. These paths treat the world as the enemy of the work, and honestly so. Their methods require a concentration that ordinary life shatters.

Second, each develops one center at the expense of the others. One-third of the being gets forged to brilliance while two-thirds stay in the dark. Gurdjieff compared the results to a man with one enormously overdeveloped limb.

Third, each takes essentially a lifetime, and its results are fragile in a specific way. What you build in a sealed environment tends to need the sealed environment. Take the monk out of the monastery, and the state that seemed permanent begins to erode — because it was held in place partly by walls.

None of this is contempt. Gurdjieff regarded all three ways as genuine — they reach the goal, for the rare person suited to them. But notice who their shared structure leaves out: almost EVERYONE. The parent. The person with work that matters. The person embedded in obligations they rightly refuse to abandon. For most of human history, the message to people like us was: your turn comes later, in another life, or never. Transformation was for renunciates. Everyone else could, at best, support the renunciates and hope.

The Fourth Way

The Fourth Way begins from a completely different premise: the conditions of your life are not the obstacle to the work. They ARE the work’s material.

No monastery. No renunciation as entry fee. You practice the Fourth Way exactly where you are standing — inside the commute, the company, the marriage, the mortgage. And not as an unfortunate compromise with reality, but because ordinary life, approached with knowledge, is a better gymnasium than the monastery. The monastery removes friction. The Fourth Way runs on it. Every irritating colleague, every crying child at 3 a.m., every traffic jam and difficult conversation is, for this way, what the barbell is to the lifter: precisely calibrated resistance, delivered fresh daily, free of charge.

Gurdjieff called it the way of the “sly man” — and that phrase deserves a little rescue from its English connotations. The sly man is not dishonest. He is EFFICIENT. Where the fakir buys his will with thirty years of suffering, the sly man understands exactly how the machine works, and produces the same result with a fraction of the effort — the way an engineer with a lever moves what a hundred laborers cannot. The slyness IS the knowledge: a precise understanding of the human machine, its centers, its energies, its leaks, its laws. That’s why this book spends so many chapters on what looks like theory. In the Fourth Way, the theory is the lever.

Three more features define the way, and each answers a defect of the traditional three:

It works on all three centers at once. Body, heart, and mind develop together, in balance — not one forged and two abandoned. A Fourth Way person should become MORE functional in ordinary life, not less: a better parent, a sharper professional, a warmer friend. In this way, lopsided development isn’t partial success. It’s deformity.

It begins from understanding rather than obedience. The traditional ways ask for submission first: do the practice for twenty years, and understanding may come. The Fourth Way inverts this. Do nothing you don’t understand, and believe nothing you haven’t verified. “Take nothing on faith” is not a slogan bolted onto the system. It is the system’s engine. Effort without understanding, Gurdjieff insisted, goes to waste — the machine simply absorbs it and continues.

And “understanding,” in this teaching, is a technical term with a precise recipe — one worth having now, because it explains something you’ve seen all your life. Gurdjieff distinguished knowledge (what a person has learned — information, theory, everything that can be written down) from being (what a person IS — their presence, their unity, the actual level of their inner development). The two are independent, and either one can outgrow the other. You’ve met the results. Knowledge far beyond being produces the brilliant fool: the professor who can lecture on psychology and can’t govern his own temper, the person who has read everything about awakening and never been present for a single breakfast. Being beyond knowledge produces its own lopsidedness: real inner substance with no framework to guide it — the “stupid saint,” in the tradition’s blunt phrase. Understanding, Gurdjieff taught, is neither one. It is the product of the two — knowledge and being, multiplied. It grows only when both grow. And this is why the Fourth Way refuses to be merely studied: reading this book adds knowledge, and knowledge alone will change nothing. Only knowledge PRACTICED — worked into being, verified in observation, paid for in effort — becomes understanding. Keep this recipe in view every time you’re tempted to substitute another chapter for another experiment.

Its results are built into life, so they survive contact with life. What crystallizes in the friction of the world doesn’t dissolve on re-entry into the world, because it never left. There is nothing to return to, and nowhere to fall from.

There’s one more difference, subtler and easy to miss. The traditional ways begin with what you must GIVE UP. The Fourth Way begins with what you must SEE. Its first demand is not sacrifice but observation — months of it, patient and unsentimental, before you try to change anything at all. You can’t repair a machine you’ve never examined. And here is the sly man’s secret: most of what needs to change begins changing under honest observation alone.

“But I Already Have a Religion”

Now for a question that stops many people right at this threshold, and it deserves a direct answer: is this a religion? Does it compete with mine?

No. And the reason is structural, not diplomatic.

Religions, whatever else they are, are primarily answers to the question WHAT: what is real, what is sacred, what happens at death, what does existence ask of us. The Fourth Way is an answer to the question HOW: how does a human being become capable of actually DOING what their religion asks? It’s a method, not a metaphysics. It has no quarrel with any sincere faith, and much to offer one.

Consider what every great tradition already demands of its practitioners, in its own vocabulary:

  • Christianity: “Watch and pray” — Christ’s instruction in Gethsemane, where the disciples’ failure was not disbelief but sleep. The entire contemplative stream of Christianity — the watchfulness (nepsis) of the Desert Fathers, the Jesus Prayer of the Eastern Church, Brother Lawrence’s practice of the presence of God — is a training in exactly what this book calls self-remembering. Gurdjieff himself, asked what his teaching was, once answered that it was esoteric Christianity.
  • Islam: muraqabah — the vigilant awareness that one lives every moment under the divine gaze; the Sufi practice of remembrance (dhikr) as an antidote to the heart’s forgetfulness (ghaflah — heedlessness, the Quran’s own diagnosis of the ordinary human condition, and as precise a word for Gurdjieff’s “sleep” as exists in any scripture).
  • Judaism: kavanah — directed presence of heart and mind in prayer and act, without which the tradition itself says the act is a body without a soul — paired with halakhah, literally “the way to go”: a complete architecture of daily practice that structures a layperson’s whole day around remembrance. And the tradition anticipates a counsel this book will repeat: when the feeling won’t come, do the practice anyway, with whatever intention you can gather — and when you drift from the path, there is always teshuva, return.
  • Buddhism: sati — mindfulness, remembering — the seventh factor of the eightfold path; and the Buddha’s final instruction: appamādena sampādetha — strive on with heedfulness.
  • Hinduism: the Gita’s teaching of action performed with full presence and without identification — work in the world, awake, as a complete spiritual path.

Every tradition, in other words, already knows that its people are asleep. Every tradition already commands them to wake. And every tradition already warns that religious activity performed mechanically — prayer recited by the machine, ritual on autopilot, scripture read on autopilot — is precisely the thing its founders came to overturn. Nor are the traditions empty of method — far from it. Halakhah structures a Jewish layperson’s entire day around remembrance; the five daily prayers do the same for a Muslim; the liturgy of the hours, for a contemplative Christian; the eightfold path is nothing if not a how. Anyone who claims religions tell you WHAT but not HOW has not looked closely. What even these structures rarely spell out, though, is the inner mechanics of the moment itself: what attention must actually DO during the prayer, why it drifts mid-blessing, what exactly has gone wrong when a sacred act runs on autopilot, and how to work with a machine that can co-opt even the holiest routine. The structures tell you when to practice, what to practice, and with what intention. What’s usually missing is the manual for the ten seconds INSIDE the practice.

That is the gap the Fourth Way fills. It doesn’t tell you what to worship. It makes you CAPABLE of worshiping — of praying a prayer you are actually present for, of practicing a commandment with the whole of yourself instead of the autopilot. A Christian who takes up this work should become more genuinely Christian. A Muslim, more genuinely Muslim. A Jew, more genuinely Jewish. If the work makes someone LESS faithful to a sincere tradition, something has gone wrong in the work.

And the reverse holds too, so let me spell it out: the Fourth Way is not hostile to the secular reader either. Its claims are framed for verification, not belief. A complete skeptic can take up every practice in this book as pure empirical psychology and lose nothing. You can enter the system from wherever you stand.

The One Truth Behind the Many Paths

Everything said so far concerns the how — the methods by which the traditions train presence. But there is a deeper layer of agreement among them, and it concerns the what: what the practitioners of all these ways, when they went all the way in, actually found. This is the second stream of this book, and it deserves its own hearing.

On the surface, the great spiritual traditions of the world look nothing alike. They use different names for God, or no name at all. They tell different stories about how we got here and what happens when we die. They prescribe different rituals, different rules, different days of rest. People have argued over these differences for thousands of years. People have killed and died over them. Look only at the surface, and you find a thousand religions that cannot agree on anything.

But go deeper. Set aside the rules and the stories and the names, and look at what the mystics of each tradition reported — those rare individuals in every age and every culture who didn’t just believe, but who went all the way in and saw for themselves. When you read their words, something strange happens. The thousand voices begin to sound like one voice. A Hindu sage, a Christian contemplative, a Taoist hermit, and a Sufi poet, separated by oceans and centuries, with no way of ever having spoken to one another, come back from the depths of their own being and describe the same discovery.

Consider what they actually said.

The ancient sages of India, in the Upanishads, reduced the whole of it to three words: Tat tvam asi — “Thou art That.” The deepest reality of the universe and the deepest reality of you are not two things. They are one. The consciousness looking out through your eyes is not separate from the consciousness looking out through mine, or through anything else.

In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna that the Self was never born and will never die — that weapons cannot cut it, fire cannot burn it. And in the same conversation, he gives the practical instruction: act, but release your grip on the fruits of your action — doing what is to be done, without resisting what is.

Those Indian scriptures grew out of Hinduism, the oldest of the living traditions, which carried the insight all the way into a complete philosophy. Its nondual school, Advaita Vedanta, holds that behind every god, form, and name there is only Brahman — one boundless consciousness — and that the world of separate things is maya: not a lie, but an appearance, the one reality shimmering as the many. This is the very “dream” we keep returning to in these pages. And the classic way of pointing home was through negation — neti, neti, “not this, not this” — setting down everything you can see, think, or feel until only the seer remains. That is exactly the move we will make later when we go looking for the self.

The Tao Te Ching opens by admitting its own limitation: the Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao. The truth cannot be put into words — only pointed at. And its central practice, wu wei, “non-doing,” is the same letting-go we will describe in meditation: not forcing, not controlling, allowing the natural way of things to move through you.

The mystical heart of the biblical tradition says it just as directly, though we are not used to reading it that way. The Hebrew Bible’s Psalms: “Be still, and know.” Not think, not believe, not strive — be still, and know. And in the Gospels, when they asked Jesus where the kingdom of God was, he did not point to a temple or a throne or the sky. He pointed within.

The mystics of Judaism mapped the same territory in their own language. At the summit of the Kabbalah stands Ein Sof — “the Endless,” the boundless reality so far beyond name and form that it can only be called the Infinite. From it all worlds pour forth in a great descent, the one light stepping down through veil after veil until it appears to harden into the separate world we know. And how does a boundless oneness make room for a universe that looks like something other than itself? Through tzimtzum, a self-contraction — the Infinite concealing itself from itself so that a world of apparent separation can arise. That is the story we will tell in “In the Beginning,” in another tongue: the one consciousness hiding its own nature so the dream can feel real. Yet the deepest teaching insists the concealment is only ever apparent — ein od milvado, “there is nothing else besides.” Behind every face and every thing, there is only the One.

The Rosicrucian Cosmo-Conception and the older esoteric streams tell it as a vast arc: spirit descending into matter, forgetting itself completely, and then slowly, across ages, waking back up and climbing home — returning to the source enriched by everything it learned along the way. Gurdjieff’s teaching, it should be said, belongs to this same esoteric current — he claimed to have gathered it from surviving schools of exactly this lineage — and its strange chemistry of energies and octaves is, at bottom, a working manual for that same arc of descent and return.

Many traditions. Many vocabularies. One finding. We are not separate selves trapped in a hostile universe. We are expressions of a single, conscious, undivided reality — and we have simply forgotten, and can remember.

Now, a word of caution, because this idea is easy to misuse. To say the traditions point at the same truth is not to say all religions are “the same,” or that their differences don’t matter, or that you should blend them into a single soup. The surface teachings genuinely differ, and those differences matter to the people who hold them. The unity is not on the surface. It is at the center — the same center we keep returning to in these pages, the one that no concept can reach. The traditions are different fingers. They point at the same moon.

And if you have no tradition at all — if the word “God” has only ever made you wince — good. You are not behind. You may even be ahead, with less to unlearn. The truth we are pointing at does not belong to any religion. It was here before all of them, and it is here right now, in you, as the very awareness reading these words.

So we will draw on the great texts throughout this book — not as authorities to obey, and not as scriptures to compete with, but as witnesses. As fellow travelers who went where we are going and left word that the path leads somewhere real. When one of them says something more beautifully than I ever could, I will let them say it.

But in the end, no text can take you there — not theirs, and certainly not this one. They can only point. You are the one who has to look, to see, to understand.

How Anyone Finds This at All

One more teaching belongs in this chapter, because it answers a question you may not have thought to ask: how did you end up holding this book?

The Work’s answer starts by sorting the influences that act on every life into three kinds. A influences are the ones created inside ordinary life: money, career, status, fashion, security, the whole churn of things pulling at everyone from every direction. They’re not evil — they’re just circular. They arise in the dream and lead back into it. B influences are different. They originate OUTSIDE ordinary life — from conscious sources — but reach us in diluted, scattered form: genuine religious teachings, real philosophy, sacred art, certain books and ideas that carry, even faintly, the taste of something from another level. Most people pass B influences their whole lives without noticing. But in some people, something recognizes them. Each encounter leaves a trace, and the traces accumulate — and slowly, over years, they form what the tradition calls a magnetic center: an inner compass, built from everything that ever whispered there is something more than this, that begins quietly steering a life. It’s the magnetic center that makes a person pick up certain books, take certain conversations seriously, feel a pull the people around them don’t feel. And if it grows strong enough, it eventually leads its owner to a C influence — direct contact with the teaching itself, undiluted, from someone who has it.

If any of this describes your own history — the strange thread of books and moments and unexplainable pulls that led, somehow, here — then you already know what a magnetic center feels like from the inside. You’ve been navigating by it for years. This chapter is simply telling you the compass has a name.

Two refinements to this teaching, both worth carrying with you. The first comes with a warning attached. Gurdjieff cautioned that a magnetic center can form BADLY — weak, or fed on imitations, unable to tell genuine B influences from clever counterfeits. And a defective compass still points somewhere: it leads its owner not to a real teaching but to a fake one — to the charlatans and imitation schools that have always surrounded the genuine article like weeds around a well. (This is exactly why Appendix A (Going Further: Finding a Work Group), at the end of this book, gives you hard tests for evaluating any group or teacher. The compass needs calibration, and the calibration is discrimination.)

The second refinement is Bennett’s, and it changes what you do with the compass now that you’ve found a teaching. In the older telling, the magnetic center’s job ends when it delivers you to the work — the guide retires at the gate. Bennett saw it differently, and I think he was right. The magnetic center doesn’t retire. It MATURES. What began as a vague pull toward certain books becomes, if it’s fed, a living faculty of discrimination — the ongoing capacity to recognize the genuine and distinguish it from the imitation, in teachings, in teachers, and eventually in YOURSELF: to tell your own real observations from your machine’s performances of observation, the true impulse from the counterfeit one. Bennett also named what makes the difference between a weak magnetic center and a strong one, and it isn’t intelligence. It’s sincerity. A compass built on curiosity alone stays a hobby. A compass built on genuine NEED — on the honest, felt recognition that something essential is missing and must be found — becomes the seed of everything that follows. He saw the magnetic center, finally, as the deepest part of a person already stirring: the first faint signal of the very self this whole book is about, reaching back toward you from ahead. Which means the pull that brought you to this page was not an accident, and not merely an interest. It was the earliest movement of the thing you’re looking for.

The Catch

It would be dishonest to end this chapter on the sales pitch, because the Fourth Way’s advantages come with a cost the traditional ways don’t impose — and Gurdjieff never hid it.

The monastery, whatever its limits, CARRIES you. The bell rings, and you pray. The rule decides, and you follow. The structure does the remembering for you. The Fourth Way has no walls, no bell, no rule. Nothing external holds the work in place. Which means exactly one thing holds the work in place: your own understanding, renewed daily, of WHY you are doing it. The moment your aim goes dim, ordinary life — the very material of the work — silently reabsorbs you. And years can pass before you notice. The way that uses sleep as its gymnasium is also the way easiest to sleep through.

This is why practitioners have always considered the Fourth Way HARDER than the three ways it improves on, even though it requires no renunciation. Harder the way freedom is harder than obedience. And it’s why the tradition insists (with an emphasis we’ll return to in Chapter 17 (The Complete Toolkit)) that no one walks this way entirely alone. Not because a group supplies belief, but because other people are the one alarm clock the machine can’t learn to sleep through.

What exactly is this machine that sleeps so skillfully? It is time to open the hood.
