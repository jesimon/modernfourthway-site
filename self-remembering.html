Self-Remembering
The Core Practice of the Fourth Way

Every camera in your house points outward. The phone, the doorbell, the laptop webcam — each one films the world and appears in none of its own footage. Scroll through a year of your photos and you’ll find everything you saw, and almost no evidence of the one who saw it.

Attention, in the ordinary machine, works exactly like this. It films the world — the road, the screen, the argument, the sunset — and the one attending appears nowhere in the footage. At the end of a day, a year, a life, there is an enormous archive of what was EXPERIENCED and almost no record of the EXPERIENCER. The camera never once turned to include itself. This is the technical structure of the sleep this book opened with. The drive home got filmed. You were not in the shot.

Self-remembering is the correction of this — and it is the center of the entire Fourth Way. Every chapter before this one exists to make it possible. Every chapter after exists to supply and sustain it. Gurdjieff said that of all the things he taught, this was the one thing that, if a person truly grasped it, contained everything else. So this chapter must be more careful than any other in the book, because self-remembering is also the most misunderstood idea in the entire tradition. People routinely mistake it for things it is not — including people who have practiced those other things for decades.

What It Is

Begin with what ordinary attention does. At any moment, attention is fused with its object — flowed out entirely into the road, the screen, the person, the thought, with nothing left behind. One arrow, pointing outward:

I → the world.

The “I” in that diagram is not experienced. It is spent — converted entirely into the experiencing, the way the camera is absent from its own footage. This total outflow is what the tradition calls identification, and it is the ordinary state: absorbed in the task, lost in the movie, consumed by the argument, gone into the daydream. Note that identification feels like nothing at all. That is its signature. It is not a sensation. It is the absence of the one who could have sensations about it.

Self-remembering is attention flowing in two directions at once:

I ↔ the world.

Aware of the road, AND aware of the one driving. Aware of the conversation, AND aware of the one listening. Aware of this page, AND — right now, as you read this sentence — aware of yourself reading it: the body in the chair, the weight, the light, the fact of your own presence here, while the reading continues. That small doubling you may have just felt — that sudden, slightly strange inclusion of yourself in the scene — is not a metaphor for self-remembering. It IS self-remembering, in a flicker. The camera just caught its own reflection.

Before going further, let’s place this state on the Work’s formal map, because the tradition names FOUR states of consciousness, and you should know all four. The first is ordinary sleep — nighttime sleep, the body horizontal, dreams running. The second is what we normally call “being awake” — up, functioning, driving, talking. The Work refuses to call this waking. It calls it waking sleep, because as Chapter 1 demonstrated, the machine runs the whole show while no one is present. This is the state nearly all of human life happens in — including, the tradition notes dryly, nearly everything history calls great and terrible. The third state is self-consciousness — not the embarrassed feeling English gives that word, but its literal meaning: consciousness OF self. Presence. Both arrows. The state this chapter teaches, which visits everyone in flashes and which the Work exists to make deliberate. And the fourth is objective consciousness — the state in which things are perceived as they actually ARE, the full reception of the higher centers described in Chapter 5. The map matters because of what it implies about the third state: it is the doorway. There is no path from waking sleep to objective consciousness that does not pass through self-remembering. Which is why everything begins here.

Hold onto the precision of this, because every counterfeit fails exactly here: both arrows, at once. Attention entirely on the world is ordinary identification. But attention entirely on yourself isn’t self-remembering either — that’s introspection, or self-consciousness, or daydreaming about yourself, all of which are just identification with a different object (the self-image, which is footage like everything else). Self-remembering is the divided state: world and self, held together, in the same moment. Ouspensky, when he finally understood this, reported the shock of realizing two things simultaneously. First, this state was obviously what the words “consciousness” and “presence” had always really meant. Second, he could not produce more than a few seconds of it — and never had, in his entire remembered life.

One more precision before the counterfeits, and it quietly changes the flavor of everything in this chapter: self-remembering is not about whether awareness is present. Awareness is ALWAYS present. It has been present in every moment of identification you have ever lost yourself in — present as the seeing of the screen, the hearing of the argument, the living of the daydream. Nothing in this chapter’s talk of losing presence and finding it again means the light goes out; no one has ever experienced the light going out, and Part VIII will make everything of that fact. What is lost and found — what this whole practice works on — is the shape of awareness: the direction of its flow.

Ordinary awareness moves like an arrow, or a river: one direction, out. The whole current pours toward its object and is spent there — which is exactly what the first diagram drew. Turn the arrow around and you have not changed anything important: attention aimed only inward is still a river, still one-way, just flowing upstream — which is why introspection fails the test above. But look closely at the second diagram, the two-headed arrow, and you will see it is really a drawing of something better drawn as a circle. In self-remembering the flow becomes circular: a whirlpool. The current still touches the world — fully, more vividly than before — but instead of dying at its target it curves, returns through the one who touches, and goes out again: world, you, world, you, one continuous turning. “Divided attention” was always a slightly misleading name; nothing is divided. One current, bent into a circle.

The image earns its keep practically. A straight flow must be pushed to include its source — that is the effort this chapter will keep demanding of you. But a whirlpool, once turning, begins to feed itself: each circulation reinforces the next. This is why the taste magnifies when the state continues, and it is the secret of the crystallization promised later in this chapter — you are not damming the river of attention; you are teaching it to turn, until the turning becomes what it does. So in the ten seconds of practice, do not hunt for more awareness. There is no more to get, and none missing. Bend the flow you already have: let some of the current that is touching the world curve back through the toucher — and when you feel the circulation close, that closing IS the state. The whirlpool is the taste.

What It Is Not

The counterfeits, in ascending order of subtlety:

It is not thinking about yourself. The sentence “I am sitting here reading” (produced by the formatory apparatus and narrated inwardly) is a thought about presence, filmed like any other object. You can narrate “I am present, I am present” for an hour while being entirely absent. The machine is perfectly capable of automating the affirmation of consciousness. The test is always the same: is there sensation of yourself — the actual weight, the actual breath, the felt fact of being here — or only commentary? Words about the taste are not the taste.

It is not self-consciousness in the social sense. The painful awareness-of-being-observed that floods you when entering a room of strangers is near-total identification — with the imagined footage of yourself in other people’s cameras. It points every arrow at the self-image and none at the actual self or the actual room. (The tradition’s name for this species of leak is internal considering — and it has a helpful twin, external considering, that reverses the arrow. Both get their own treatment in Chapter 11 (Working with the Body and Emotions).)

It is not concentration or flow. This is the misunderstanding that catches the most capable people. Deep absorption in coding, climbing, painting, playing (the flow state, rightly prized) is attention magnificently unified in one direction: pure, high-quality identification. The proof is in what flow’s own researchers report as its defining feature: self-consciousness disappears, and hours pass unnoticed. The camera has never been more perfectly aimed outward. Flow is a superb state of the machine — and no one is home, in a specific, checkable sense. Ask the flowing person afterward for the felt fact of their own existence during the third hour, and they will tell you, accurately, that they weren’t there. Self-remembering can coexist with skilled action — that is precisely the aim — but it is the second arrow that makes the difference, and flow, by its nature, retracts it.

And it is not — quite — mindfulness as commonly taught. Care here, because the overlap is real and the traditions are family. Mindfulness practice in its popular form trains sustained, non-judgmental attention on an object — breath, body, sounds, the present task. This is genuine training and genuinely valuable. It builds the attentional musculature everything else requires, and this book will borrow it freely. But watch the geometry: attention on the breath is still one arrow — a calmer, steadier, more wholesome arrow, pointed at a subtler object. And people can, and usually do, practice it in the same self-absent mode as everything else: twenty peaceful minutes of breath-following from which the meditator was, in the two-arrow sense, missing. (Honesty requires the footnote: the deeper strata of the contemplative traditions know the second arrow well — the Buddhist teachings on sampajañña, clear comprehension of oneself in activity, are pointing at very nearly this — but what reaches the app and the corporate workshop is almost always single-arrow training.) The Fourth Way’s contribution is not a better object of attention. It is the divided geometry itself.

The Taste of It

The counterfeits above are tests you can run — but a checklist of negatives is not enough, because the machine passes written exams. It is remarkably, humiliatingly easy to believe self-remembering is happening when it is not: the idea of the state standing in for the state, the formatory echo saying the right words in an empty room. So do not rely on the idea of self-remembering. Rely on the taste — and this section exists so you can learn it.

Here is the honest phenomenology. When self-remembering actually begins, it does not feel like much of anything — no fireworks, no altered state, nothing to write home about. Just a slight shift: a little more awake, a little more here. The shift is so modest that beginners routinely dismiss it while hunting for something grander — or accept a counterfeit instead, because the counterfeit at least announces itself. Notice the asymmetry, because it is the whole diagnostic: the fake produces an announcement; the real produces a shift.

But stay with the real thing — not by clutching, just by remaining, both arrows open — and after a short while the shift magnifies. Something clarifies. Perception comes cleaner; the moment becomes more specific; the fact of being here stops being an assertion and becomes unmistakable. A distinct kind of clarity can be felt when self-remembering is proceeding properly — and once you have felt it, you will not confuse it with its imitations again. This is the criterion. When the clarity is present, the practice is working. When only the sentence “I am present” is present, it is not — no matter how sincerely the sentence is being said.

Ouspensky noticed something further that you can verify over months: moments of genuine self-remembering are recognizable in memory by their distinct flavor. Years later you can still locate them — that street corner, that face, that four seconds — standing out from the surrounding film like the only frames you were actually in. The counterfeits leave no taste at all, which is how you know, in retrospect, what they were.

So make the calibration deliberate, once, today. First produce the counterfeit on purpose: say inwardly “I am here, I am present” while remaining exactly as absent as usual. Feel what that is — words, and nothing else. Then drop the words and actually arrive: the weight of the body, the light, the sound, the one who is reading. Taste the difference between the two. The words are the label on the bottle; the arrival is the drink. Memorize the drink. That memorized taste becomes your instrument for the whole path — the one thing in this practice the machine cannot forge.

Why It Is So Hard

Now for the finding that turns this from a technique into an abyss — the same finding as Chapter 1’s ten-minute experiment, which you have now felt for yourself: THE STATE CANNOT BE HELD. Two or three seconds, perhaps ten with effort, and the second arrow silently retracts. Attention fuses with the next object. And (this is the crucial part) no one ever witnesses the retraction itself. You do not experience losing self-remembering. You experience, some minutes later, the small jolt of recovering it, with a gap in between that was simply not attended by anyone. Sleep does not announce itself. It is discovered only in retrospect, from inside the next waking.

Why? The machine’s own architecture answers, and by now you own every piece of the answer:

The chemistry runs dry. Divided attention is the most expensive operation the machine can perform. It runs exclusively on the refinery’s finest fraction — the trace product that ordinary functioning flares off daily in negative emotion, tension, and churn. The ten-minute experiment failed the way a car with a cupful of fuel fails: not from weak resolve but from an empty tank. This is the single most liberating reframe in the entire Work. The inability to stay present is NOT a character flaw. It is an energy problem — and energy problems have engineering solutions.

Nothing in the machine is asking for it. Every center is fully occupied and perfectly content running single-arrow. The autopilots are specifically designed to operate without presence — that is their function — and they are magnificent at it. Self-remembering has no constituency in the machinery. No habit supports it, no stimulus triggers it, nothing reminds you. You must insert it against the grain of everything, from a place the machine does not contain. (This is why the tradition insists upon alarm clocks, as Chapter 17 (The Complete Toolkit) will explain — and why Gurdjieff structured so much of his teaching as the deliberate manufacture of reminders.)

And the saboteur is intimate. The machine defends its sleep — not malevolently, but the way any automated system routes around interference. Its most elegant defense: producing a simulation of the practice. Within days of beginning, the formatory apparatus learns the vocabulary and starts supplying “I am remembering myself” as a cached thought at plausible intervals. The moving parts learn to generate a faint gesture-toward-presence that satisfies the intention without containing anyone. Every practitioner discovers this counterfeit in themselves. The discovery is a milestone, not a failure. You are watching the machine do the only thing it can do — automate — to the one thing that cannot be automated.

So here’s the honest arithmetic of the beginning: a sincere person, working alone, remembers themselves in genuine flickers — a few seconds at a time, a few times a day, mostly by accident — against a background of hours of unbroken absence. Gurdjieff put it bluntly: man cannot remember himself. The state visits, and the machine cannot summon the visitor. If that were the end of the analysis, the Work would be a counsel of despair. But it’s not the end — because flickers, rightly used, COMPOUND. And you’ve already seen the mechanism of compounding.

The Loop, Entered

Recall Chapter 4 (The Human Biological Machine): impressions received in a moment of presence yield multiples of the fine energy that the same impressions yield when received by an absent machine — and fine energy is the fuel of presence itself. There is the whole strategy, visible in one sentence. You cannot hold self-remembering by force. But each flicker of it, however brief, is a moment of dramatically increased fuel production. The fuel makes the next flicker slightly more available. And the leaks you plug (each one an act of observation that is itself a flicker) stop the daily hemorrhage of exactly this fuel. The state that cannot be sustained can be fed. Presence is grown, not gripped — cultivated the way soil fertility is cultivated, by many small returns, not seized in one heroic season.

Practically, then, the beginner’s work is not “stay present” (an instruction the machine cannot execute) but three humbler operations it can:

Multiply the flickers. Since the machine contains no reminder, install reminders in the world — and here the sly man does his signature move. Choose ordinary, unavoidable events and appoint them alarm clocks: every doorway you pass through today — arrive, both arrows, for the two seconds of the threshold. Every red light. Every time the phone buzzes — and savor the judo of this one. The attention economy has spent billions engineering interruptions that seize your attention a hundred times a day. This practice re-purposes each seizure as a summons: BEFORE looking at the screen, take one breath, feel the weight of the body, the fact of being here, the fact of being the one about to look. The notification becomes a bell of mindfulness paid for by the very industry of distraction. Taste hearing it that way just once, and the world stops being the enemy of presence and becomes its infrastructure. Any recurring event will serve: hands under running water, sitting down, the first sip of coffee, your own name in a meeting. Rotate them. The machine automates any single alarm within a week or two (you will watch it happen — instructive in itself), and rotation stays ahead of the automation.

Don’t lengthen — deepen. When a flicker comes, the reflex is to clutch (to try to stretch it) and the clutch itself is a thought that ends the state. Instead, for the two seconds it lasts, make it fuller: sensation of the whole body, the light, the sound, the fact of existing, all received at once, both arrows wide. A deep two seconds outproduces a strained thirty. The duration will grow by itself, from chemistry, on a timescale of months. It is not your department.

Harvest the returns. The jolt of coming back (the “oh, I was gone”) arrives, for everyone, wrapped in a flash of discouragement, and the discouragement (note its source: negative part of the emotional center, right on schedule) burns the very fuel the return just generated. Train one substitution, and let me say it as clearly as I can, because it may be the most important instruction in this book: when you realize you’ve forgotten (and you will, a thousand times) do not criticize yourself. The moment of realizing “I forgot” is itself a moment of waking up. It’s not the failure. It’s the success, arriving a beat late. So instead of turning that moment into self-reproach (which is just more sleep), simply use it — treat every “oh, I drifted off again” as a friendly bell ringing you awake, and gently return. Forgetting, noticing, returning — that loop is not the obstacle to the practice. That loop is the practice. You cannot catch yourself falling asleep. You can only be the one who wakes — and each waking, greeted as a waking rather than mourned as a failure, is a deposit. The practitioner’s day is measured not in presence sustained but in number of returns — and that number responds to work within weeks.

Tend the instrument. The first engineering solution to the energy problem is embarrassingly simple, and the machine’s pride will be tempted to skip it: it will be difficult to do this work properly while the body has unmet needs. An overtired body, a hungry body, is not a neutral platform for divided attention — it is a body broadcasting, and the instinctive center’s alarms capture the arrow faster than any thought can. The carriage metaphor already told you this: no driver holds a route behind a starving horse — and no autopilot holds one while the battery is depleted. So check in with the body regularly — it takes five seconds and pairs naturally with the body-arrivals you are already practicing. Tired? If a nap is possible, take the nap; twenty minutes of sleep buys more presence than two hours of gray-faced trying. Hungry? Have the snack. This is not indulgence; it is maintenance — and the Fourth Way, unlike the fakir’s, has never once asked you to practice against a tormented instrument. With the body’s basic needs met, you will find it far easier to remain centered, and the whirlpool holds its turn on a fraction of the effort. Two honesties keep this clean. When a need genuinely cannot be met — the middle of the shift, the red-eye, the night feed — the need itself becomes the second arrow: aware of the tiredness AND the world; the practice continues, just gentler. And watch the machine, which will convert this paragraph into a permission slip within the week — “too tired to practice” from the couch, the fourth snack as avoidance. The test is simple and felt: meeting a real need quiets the body. Indulging an impulse loudens it.

Keep the aim warm. The strength of your aim is everything here. The more deeply you want to wake up (the more it genuinely matters to you) the more often you’ll remember. A weak wish produces a few scattered moments. A strong aim, held over time, produces a life increasingly lit from within by presence. So remind yourself, daily, why you’re doing this. (This is not motivational decoration. It is octave engineering, as Part IV will make precise — the daily renewal of aim is the shock that keeps the whole enterprise from bending.)

And hold the shape of this work without illusion, because the shape IS the work: self-remembering will be lost. Again and again — not as a beginner’s problem that advanced practice outgrows, but as the standing condition of the path, for years. And so the practice, in its entirety, is this: find your way back. Again. And again. And again. Not once, heroically, but times beyond counting, ordinarily — until the returning itself wears a channel, and the channel becomes a riverbed, and what flows there begins to flow on its own. Be suspicious of anyone who tells you they simply stayed present; they are describing a state they imagined. Everyone who ever woke, woke back.

And be patient, because this is slow work and cumulative work. At first the moments of self-remembering are tiny islands in a vast sea of sleep — a few seconds here, a few seconds there, scattered across a day otherwise lived mechanically. That’s fine. That’s how it begins for everyone. But every single moment of genuine self-remembering leaves a trace. The effect accumulates, quietly, beneath the surface, over months and years. Slowly the islands grow. Slowly the remembering comes more often and more easily. Where that slow growth finally leads — and what becomes of effort itself when it arrives — is the last thing this chapter owes you, stated plainly.

The Effort — and the End of Effort

First, let’s talk about the cost. Self-remembering is real work, and it is hard — not hard the way calculus is hard, but hard the way swimming upstream is hard: it goes against what the machine wants, all of it, all the time. No center asks for it. No habit supports it. Every current in you runs the other way, and each act of dividing attention is performed against the unanimous vote of everything automatic in you. And the efforts must be repeated — not for a weekend, not for the length of a workshop’s glow, but for months and years: efforts made when nothing rewards them, made again after the thousandth forgetting, made on the days the whole enterprise feels invented. The Work does not apologize for this, and neither will this book. In this tradition nothing real is given without payment, and effort is the currency.

Now the horizon, with equal honesty: not forever. Effort is the scaffold, not the building. What is practiced long enough and fed long enough stops being an act and becomes structure — Chapter 7 (Center of Gravity) gave the process its name: crystallization. When presence crystallizes, self-remembering stops being something you do and becomes something you are. It arrives unbidden. It sustains itself. The state that once cost everything to produce now costs nothing to maintain — natural, effortless, the way you make no effort to know your own name. The islands have become the mainland, and no one is treading water anymore. Effort can then be dropped, not as a compromise but as a completion — the scaffold coming down because the building stands.

One guard, because the machine has read this section too. It will file for effortlessness early. Three weeks in, a voice will observe that surely by now the practice should flow by itself, that continued strain is a sign of doing it wrong, that the wise thing is to relax. That is not crystallization speaking; that is the negotiation — sleep’s lawyer, quoting the tradition back at you. The real sign is never that effort has grown tiresome. The real sign is that presence keeps arriving before you reach for it — unarranged, again and again, on its own schedule — and even then, the ones who have crossed that line report that they kept making efforts long after the efforts became unnecessary, out of gratitude, the way you might keep visiting the teacher after the lessons end.

So the instruction for now — and “now” here means years — is simple and unsoftened: make the effort. Real effort, today, against the grain, with the taste of the genuine state as your compass and the thousandth return as your metric. The effortlessness at the end is real, and it is purchased by nothing else.

What Shows Up

One promise, from the tradition’s collective experience, so that you recognize the early results and are not talked out of them by their modesty. The first fruits of self-remembering are not fireworks. They are moments of ordinary life arriving in unaccountable high resolution. A stretch of street between two doorways, seen (actually seen) for four seconds, with a vividness that startles. The taste of coffee, once that week, tasted by someone. A beloved, familiar face suddenly there, specific and real and slightly miraculous, across the ordinary table. These moments feel disproportionately significant, and the feeling is accurate. They are samples of what the higher centers’ broadcast sounds like when, for a moment, the receiver comes online. They are what the glass from Chapter 1 looks like from the other side.

And they raise a question this book will answer in Part IV. If flickers are possible, what governs their growth into something continuous? Why does every effort in this direction (every effort in any direction) start bright and then bend, stall, and quietly die unless something specific intervenes? The tradition’s answer is that all processes, inner and outer, unfold under two great laws — and that the sly man’s real advantage, the knowledge that replaces the monastery’s walls, is knowing exactly where every process breaks and what to place there. Those laws are coming.

But first, self-remembering has three companions — three more core practices that feed it, deepen it, and catch what it cannot. Self-remembering divides attention in the thick of life, eyes open and hands full. The next practice does something complementary: it opens attention completely, in stillness, and lets everything be.

Practice: Self-Remembering

  1. Right now, wherever you are, keep doing what you’re doing — but add yourself to it. Stay aware of the task or the surroundings, and become aware of your own presence, here, doing it. Hold both at once.
  2. Don’t narrate it in words (“I am doing this”). Just feel, wordlessly, that you are here. A quiet sense of your own being, running alongside the activity.
  3. Once today, calibrate against the counterfeit. Say inwardly “I am here” while staying exactly as absent as usual — feel that. Then drop the words and actually arrive, both arrows. Taste the difference, and memorize the taste of the real one: a slight shift toward clarity that grows if you stay.
  4. Check in with the body a few times today — five seconds each. Tired? Take the nap if you can. Hungry? Have the snack. A body whose basic needs are met holds the divided state far more easily; a body running on empty drags every arrow to itself.
  5. Pick one ordinary, frequent event to use as a reminder for the next few days — walking through a doorway, picking up your phone, sitting down. Each time it happens, let it ring you awake: remember yourself.
  6. You will forget for long stretches. Every time you notice you forgot, don’t criticize yourself — that noticing is the waking up. Simply smile, and return. And when it is lost again — and it will be — find your way back again. This, repeated, is the whole practice.
  7. Keep your aim warm. The more it matters to you to be awake, the more often you’ll remember. Brief moments, many times a day, are exactly right. The effect builds over months and years — and the effort it takes now will not be needed forever.
