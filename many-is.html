The Modern Fourth Way · From Chapter 9

The Crowd Inside
Many I's, Buffers, and Conscience

Before we leave the schematic and turn to practice, there is one more discovery waiting — and it may be the most personal one in the entire Work. Everything so far has mapped the machine’s PARTS: the foods, the centers, the compartments, the type, the hidden hub. This chapter maps something stranger. It asks: who is operating all of this? And the answer the Work gives is the one nobody wants to hear.

Gurdjieff had a favorite picture for the whole situation, and it’s worth having in full, because it holds this entire chapter in one image. A human being, he said, is like a horse-drawn carriage. The carriage is the body. The horse is the emotions. The driver is the mind. And the master — the owner, the one the whole rig exists to serve — is supposed to be seated inside, giving the destination.

Now look at the actual state of the rig. The carriage hasn’t been maintained — it creaks with tension, its joints stiff from disuse and misuse. The horse was never properly educated — nobody ever trained the emotions, remember, so it bolts at shadows and pulls toward every attractive smell. The driver is asleep on his box, or drunk in the tavern, or chatting with other drivers — and when he does drive, he goes wherever the last passenger told him, or wherever the horse pulls. And the master? The seat is EMPTY. The carriage rattles through the streets, day after day, year after year, driven by whoever climbs in and shouts an address.

A century later, the rig has updated itself, and the situation has gotten stranger. Picture a self-driving car. The vehicle is the body — it mostly runs, though nobody’s kept up the scheduled maintenance. The battery is the emotions: the power source everything else draws on, temperamental in ways any owner learns — it drains fast in the cold, surges when it’s fed, and floods the dashboard with warnings the moment it runs low. The computer systems that run the autopilot and navigation are the mind — and yes, autopilot is the same word this book has been using since its first page, now driving an actual car: software trained on millions of miles of other people’s driving, magnificent at lane-keeping, signaling, merging, stopping at reds. And the owner — the one the whole machine exists to serve, the only one authorized to enter a destination — is in the back seat. Asleep.

Look at this rig on the road. It performs beautifully: glides through traffic, obeys every light, threads construction zones that would rattle a human driver. Anyone watching would say: now there is a machine that knows what it’s doing. And it circles the same streets all day, because the one field the car cannot fill by itself is Destination, and the one being who could fill it hasn’t opened his eyes since the trip began. Miles accumulate; the battery spends itself; the odometer climbs and the journey never starts. Gurdjieff’s carriage made the same point with an empty seat. The modern rig makes it more precisely — and more uncomfortably: the master isn’t missing. He’s aboard, gently rocked by his own machinery, sleeping through a ride that was never once his.

One honest note about the trade. Every modern machine is an upgrade on the carriage except in one place: the horse. A battery drains and charges; a horse wants. It bolts at shadows, refuses bridges, forms attachments, can be ruined by one cruel handler or transformed by a patient one — which is exactly the emotional center as you’ve already met it. On this one point the old analogy remains the better psychology, and it’s worth keeping both rigs in mind: the self-driving car for what the mind has become, the horse for what the emotions have never stopped being.

Notice what this picture adds to everything we’ve built. Each part of the rig needs its own kind of care — the carriage needs maintenance (the body work coming in Chapter 11), the horse needs education (which is why the emotional center, that supercomputer never sent to school, gets so much attention in this book), and the driver needs to WAKE UP. But the deepest point is the empty seat. The rig doesn’t need a better driver. It needs its OWNER. And the whole of the Work — every practice in this book — is the process by which the master returns to the carriage.

But who climbs in and shouts the addresses, in the meantime? That’s where this chapter really begins.

The Many I’s

Here is the discovery: there is no single “I” running your life. There is a CROWD.

We assume — it’s the deepest assumption we have — that behind our thoughts and actions stands one person, one “I,” continuous from morning to night and year to year. The Work says: look. Actually look, with the observation skills you’ve been building. What you’ll find is that the machine contains hundreds of small I’s, taking turns at the controls. Each one is partial. Each one arises from some compartment of some center, summoned by circumstances. And here is the trick that hides the whole show: each one, while it’s on stage, calls itself “I” — speaks with your voice, signs your name, claims to be the whole of you. Then circumstances shift, another I takes the stage, and it does exactly the same thing.

You have a mountain of evidence for this already. It’s just been filed under other names.

The I that sets the alarm for 5:30 a.m., full of resolve, is not the I that turns it off at 5:30 a.m. Those are two different I’s, with different wants, and the second one doesn’t feel bound by ANYTHING the first one promised — why would it? It wasn’t there. The I that swears off the argument is not the I that restarts it an hour later. The I that vows honesty in the morning is not the I that shades the truth at the afternoon meeting. We call this “weakness” or “hypocrisy,” and we suffer over it. The Work calls it simple arithmetic: an I can only bind itself. It cannot bind the others. A resolution made by one member of the crowd is a promise the other several hundred never signed.

And this discovery reaches both backward and forward through this book. Backward, it finally explains Gurdjieff’s most radical claim from Chapter 1 — that a sleeping being cannot DO anything. Here is its mechanism. Doing requires one will, held over time. A crowd has no single will. It has a parade of small wills, each in charge for minutes, each undoing the others’ work. “Man cannot do” because there is no MAN there yet — only the crowd, and the empty seat. And forward: when you reach the Law of Octaves (Chapter 13), you’ll learn why every intention lawfully bends partway to its goal. Part of that answer is already in your hands. The I that launches a project is not the one at the controls when the hard middle arrives. A DIFFERENT I is on duty at week three — one that never wanted the gym membership in the first place.

Please don’t take this as philosophy. Take it as an observation assignment, because it’s one of the most verifiable claims in the whole system. For the next few days, start noticing the handoffs. Catch the moment when the I that was present at breakfast is clearly no longer the one answering email at ten. Notice how completely each I believes it is all of you — and how completely it forgets the others’ promises. Just watching this, honestly, changes your relationship to your own resolutions forever. (And it makes you gentler with everyone else’s, too.)

Buffers

Now, an obvious question — and it’s the right one to ask. If we really are a crowd of contradictory I’s, making and breaking each other’s promises all day long, why don’t we NOTICE? The contradictions are enormous. One I is generous and one is petty; one is brave and one is a coward; one says “I never lie” and another lies within the hour. If we saw all of this at once, in full view, the shock would be unbearable. So why do we feel, all day, like one reasonably consistent person?

Because the machine has shock absorbers. Gurdjieff called them buffers.

On a train, buffers are the devices between the cars that absorb the shock of impact, so the cars can bang together without shattering. Psychological buffers do exactly the same thing: they are automatic mechanisms that keep our contradictory I’s from ever colliding in awareness. The generous I and the petty I live in the same machine, sometimes minutes apart — and the buffer between them ensures they NEVER MEET. Each operates in its own compartment, unaware of the other, and the feeling of being one consistent person sails on undisturbed.

You cannot see your own buffers directly — that’s their whole function — but you can see them at work in the wild, and once you know what to look for, you’ll see them everywhere. The person who explains, with real feeling, “I’m just a very honest person,” minutes after telling a social lie they didn’t even register. The relative whose politics and whose personal conduct contradict each other completely, with no apparent friction. YOU, wincing at someone else’s hypocrisy while — and this is a promise — hosting equivalent contradictions of your own that you have literally never noticed. Everyone else’s buffers are visible. That’s what makes ours so hard to believe in.

Understand why buffers exist, because it matters. They aren’t stupidity, and they aren’t exactly lies. They grew, automatically, because a full view of our own contradictions would be genuinely unbearable to the unprepared psyche. They’re a mercy, of a kind. But the mercy has a terrible price: a buffered machine cannot change. You cannot reconcile contradictions you cannot see. You cannot wake up while an automatic system is smoothing over every jolt that would have woken you. And here is the connection to Chapter 8: buffers are the bodyguards of chief feature. The reason your central habit is invisible to you and obvious to your friends is that a wall of buffers stands around it, absorbing every observation that comes near.

This is why the Work’s whole first phase is observation, and why it insists the observation be uncomfortable. Every honest self-observation is a small collision — two I’s meeting without the buffer, a contradiction seen bare. It stings. It’s SUPPOSED to sting. The Work doesn’t remove buffers by force (that would be surgery without anesthesia); it lets the light of observation dissolve them slowly, collision by survivable collision. The photographs you’ve been taking since Chapter 8? Some of them are already doing this. The ones that stung most were the ones that landed between the cars.

Conscience

And now the question underneath everything, the one this whole chapter has been walking toward. What happens as the buffers dissolve? What is actually UNDER them?

Gurdjieff’s answer is the most hopeful thing he ever taught, and he was not a man given to hopeful teachings.

Under the buffers lies conscience.

Be careful with the word, because he did not mean what we usually mean. What we call conscience — the internalized voice of parents and culture, different in every society, telling us which fork to use and which behaviors bring shame — the Work files under personality. It’s learned, local, and mostly mechanical. Gurdjieff meant something else: an innate faculty, identical in every human being on earth, buried but never destroyed — the capacity to FEEL, all at once, the whole truth about oneself. Not to think it. To feel it. He described conscience as a state in which a person feels, together and simultaneously, everything they know about themselves — every contradiction the buffers have been keeping apart, experienced at once, with full emotional force.

That sounds unbearable, and at first it is — which is exactly why the buffers grew. But here is the teaching’s turn. That same fire, voluntarily endured, is the one force strong enough to do what nothing else in the machine can do: fuse the crowd into one. The many I’s, brought into a single feeling-awareness of each other, begin to melt together — the way separate pieces of metal, held in one flame, become one piece. Later in this book, Chapter 19 (The Second Conscious Shock) will show how suffering consciously borne becomes the very substance of being. When you get there, remember this moment — because the awakening of conscience is where that transformation gets its material. This is the Work’s actual answer to the question “how does a permanent I ever form?” Not by willpower. Not by choosing your favorite I and promoting it. By conscience — the felt truth of the whole crowd, endured awake — welding what was many into what is one.

And notice something beautiful: because objective conscience is the same in every human being, the morality that grows from it isn’t cultural preference. A person in whom conscience has awakened doesn’t refrain from harming others because their society disapproves. They refrain because they FEEL the truth of what harm is — directly, the way you feel heat. Part VIII will arrive at the same place from the other direction, when it shows that the person in front of you is not actually separate from you. Two paths, one destination: what the direct path calls love, the Work calls awakened conscience. Same fire. It always is.

Practice: Meeting the Crowd

  1. For three days, log the handoffs. Whenever you notice that a clearly different I has taken the controls — different mood, different wants, different opinions than an hour ago — note it in one line: what left, what arrived, what changed hands.
  2. Collect one broken promise, without self-attack. Find a small resolution that didn’t survive, and write down honestly: which I made it, and which I broke it? Practice saying “the I that promised isn’t the I that was there” — not as an excuse, but as a diagnosis.
  3. Hunt one buffer — in yourself, gently. Take one criticism from your Chapter 8 logs that stung disproportionately, and ask: what two things in me is this observation trying to bring into contact? Don’t force an answer. Just hold the question.
  4. Once this week, when an observation stings, DON’T reach for the explanation, the justification, or the counter-example. Let it sting for ten full seconds, felt in the body. That sting, voluntarily endured, is the first spark of what the Work means by conscience — and it is precious.

And with that, the schematic phase of this book is truly complete: the foods, the centers, the parts, the type, the hub — and now the crowd, its shock absorbers, and the buried judge beneath them. What remains is the thing the whole schematic exists for — the act that all of it serves, the one Gurdjieff placed at the center of the entire Work, beside which everything else is preparation.

It is time to talk about self-remembering.
