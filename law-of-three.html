The Law of Three
Why Every Effort Meets Resistance

Why do arguments never end?

Not the trivial ones — those end when someone gets hungry. I mean the real ones. The argument you’ve had with your partner, in some form, for years. The political argument your country has been having for generations. The argument between departments at work that outlives every reorganization and every person originally involved. Two positions, each with force, each with intelligent defenders, pushing against each other — and producing, decade after decade, nothing new. Not victory, not synthesis, not even exhaustion. Just the same two forces, locked, generating heat.

Common sense says one side must eventually win, or a compromise must split the difference. Observation says otherwise: the lock is stable. Push harder, and the resistance stiffens proportionally. Anyone who has ever tried to win a marriage argument by escalating knows this mechanism personally. Two forces do not resolve. Two forces PERPETUATE.

Gurdjieff’s teaching begins its cosmology (and its most practical everyday tool) from exactly this observation, elevated to a universal principle. He called it the Law of Three: every real event, every genuine arising of anything new, requires three forces, not two. Nothing in the universe (from chemistry to psychology to the fate of your resolutions) happens from the meeting of two forces alone. Where you see only two, either nothing is happening (however loud the noise), or a third force is present that you have not seen. And the failure to see third force, Gurdjieff said, is one of the marks of our sleep. We perceive the world as pairs (cause and effect, for and against, effort and obstacle) and then we are perpetually mystified that our two-force models keep failing to predict or produce anything.

The Three Forces

The three forces have technical names in the tradition — affirming, denying, and reconciling (or active, passive, and neutralizing) — but the names have caused a century of misunderstanding, so let’s start with their functions instead:

The first force is what pushes — the initiative, the intention, the desire, the effort. In your resolution to exercise, it is the resolve itself. In a startup, the founder’s drive. In a seed, the germinating impulse.

The second force is what resists — and here comes the first correction of ordinary thinking. Resistance is not the enemy of the event. It is a requirement of it. The second force is not evil, not failure, not the world being uncooperative. It is the density of reality — the fact that materials have properties, that habits have inertia, that other people have their own first forces. The seed’s second force is the soil: the very thing that resists the shoot is the thing that holds the root. Without second force, first force does not act on anything. It disperses, like a push against air. (Sit with how much this single reframe changes: the resistance you meet in any undertaking is not evidence the undertaking is wrong. It is evidence the undertaking is real — it has engaged something. A plan meeting no resistance has not yet touched the world.)

The third force is what relates them — and this is the one the ordinary mind cannot see. It is not a compromise between the first two, not a referee, not more push. It is a different kind of thing: the context, medium, catalyst, or higher factor in whose presence the first two forces, instead of locking, combine into a result. In chemistry it is the catalyst — the substance that participates in the reaction without being consumed by it, in whose presence two chemicals that would sit inert together for a thousand years combine in seconds. In the seed it is sunlight and season. In the deadlocked negotiation, it’s the deadline, or the mediator, or the sudden appearance of a common threat — notice how instantly an outside competitor “resolves” the feud between two departments that no amount of internal argument could touch. Same two forces. A third arrives. The lock becomes a process.

Gurdjieff’s claim, again, is that this is not a psychological heuristic but a law running through everything. He called the triad the fundamental structure of all creation, and located it, pointedly, at the root of the trinities that appear in virtually every theology on earth. This book will stay close to the ground where you can verify it. But keep in the corner of your eye the size of the claim being made.

Reading Your Own Deadlocks

Now the practical instrument. Take any place in your life where effort has been applied (sincerely, repeatedly, sometimes for years) and nothing changes. The diet. The book you are always about to write. The conversation you keep almost having with your father. The habit you have quit eleven times. Run the three-force analysis:

Name the first force. Usually easy: the intention, and it is usually genuine. (People beat themselves up for weak first force — “I just don’t want it enough” — and are usually wrong. Wanting is rarely the missing ingredient. Observe how much wanting has already been spent.)

Name the second force. Also usually visible once you look without moralizing: the inertia of the existing habit, the pleasure of the thing being renounced, the fatigue at day’s end, the fear underneath the procrastination, the other person’s own first force pointed the other way. Name it as a force (a real, legitimate, structural feature of the situation) rather than as a moral failing or an enemy. This step alone changes the emotional economics of the whole problem: the war on the second force ends, and with it the negative emotion that war was burning as fuel.

Now look for the third — and find its absence. In virtually every honest analysis of a stuck personal project, this is the finding: two forces, faithfully deadlocked, and no third force anywhere in the structure. The resolve pushes. The habit resists. And nothing in the situation relates them. And now you can see, with terrible clarity, why the universal instinctive strategy — PUSH HARDER — has failed eleven times and will fail the twelfth. Adding force to the first force does not create a third force. It creates a stronger deadlock. The resistance recruits proportionally: the diet gets stricter, and the binge gets bigger. The argument gets louder, and the defenses get deeper. The whole system returns to equilibrium at a higher level of strain. More first force is the answer the machine always proposes, because the machine sees in pairs. It is almost never the answer.

The sly man’s move (and by now you can feel the shape of the sly man’s moves) is to stop pushing and engineer the third force. What does that look like? It looks like whatever changes the relationship between push and resistance without simply amplifying the push:

  • The training partner at 6 a.m. — not because they add force, but because the appointment restructures the field in which resolve meets bed. (Why does accountability work when willpower fails? This law is why. The partner is not first force. The partner is context.)
  • The environment redesign — the phone charging in the kitchen, the guitar out of its case on a stand, the junk food simply not in the house. Third force par excellence: nothing pushes harder, nothing resists less, and the outcome changes because the medium changed.
  • The reframe that dissolves a marriage argument in one sentence — the moment both parties suddenly see the common thing they are both protecting. No position changed. A third element entered. And the two forces began, for the first time, to combine instead of collide.
  • The deadline, the public commitment, the paid course, the group — all of them, seen structurally, third-force machinery.

Awareness as Third Force

Everything so far has engineered third force from the outside — the deadline, the training partner, the empty parking lot. Good engineering, and you should do it. But the tradition has a sharper point, and it is the reason this chapter sits in a book about waking up rather than a book about productivity: you carry a third force with you. In inner work, the neutralizing force par excellence is consciousness itself.

First, be clear about what it is not. It is not willpower. Willpower is the machine fighting the machine — one more battalion sent to reinforce first force, which is why the war gets louder and the stalemate gets stronger, and why every white-knuckle victory is followed, with such suspicious reliability, by a collapse. Adding force to one side of a two-force deadlock does not create a triad. It creates a bigger deadlock.

Now watch what awareness does instead, in the smallest case: the impulse to check the phone (first force) meets the intention not to (second force) — an unwinnable two-force war you have lost ten thousand times. Add observation — the flicker of self-remembering in which someone present sees both the impulse and the intention at once — and something genuinely strange happens, which you can verify this week. The deadlock does not intensify. It loosens. Not because the watcher pushes (the watcher pushes nothing), but because the two forces, held in one awareness, are for the first time in relation. Presence is not a third combatant. It is the room in which the combat can become something else.

Run a larger case, at ten o’clock some evening, standing in the kitchen: the craving (first force) against the resolution (second force), the familiar loop already warming up. Bring divided attention — the craving felt honestly in the body, the resolution seen without a sermon, and you, present to both. Sometimes the craving simply unwinds; deprived of a war to feed on, it turns out to be mostly noise. Sometimes you eat the thing — but awake, tasting it, by something that resembles choice. Notice that in either case something NEW occurred: the mechanical loop did not run. A triad completed where a stalemate used to live. That is not a metaphor for transformation. On the tradition’s account, it is transformation, at its smallest working scale.

This is why the Work insists, with such stubbornness, that observation must come before change — and why change attempted without observation is really just first force recruiting a new slogan. It is also a preview of machinery you will meet again. When Chapter 16 shows presence entering the body’s chemistry at the exact point where an impression arrives, that is this same act under its technical name — the first conscious shock. And when the enneagram appears in Chapter 15, watch where the triangle touches the circle: third force entering a process precisely at the points where the process’s own momentum fails. You are learning that figure’s secret three chapters early.

So hold the practical summary in one line: you cannot always summon the missing deadline, the partner, the parking lot — but self-remembering IS third force, the one that is always in stock. To do the Work, at bottom, is to become the neutralizing force in the triads of your own life. Everything else in this book is technique for doing exactly that.

The Forces Are Not Fixed

One more turn of the instrument, and it is the subtlest: the three forces are roles, not identities. Nothing is inherently first, second, or third force. Everything depends on the event in question, and the same element changes roles as the situation changes. Your habit of rising early is first force in the project of writing, second force in the project of late evenings with your partner. The colleague who resists your proposal is second force in this meeting and, properly enlisted, third force in the next — the one whose skepticism, once it is inside the plan, is exactly what relates your ambition to reality. The tradition trains this perception deliberately. In any situation, ask not “who is with me and who is against me” (the machine’s binary, straight from the formatory apparatus) but “what is pushing, what is resisting, and what (present or absent) relates them?” Asked honestly, the question rearranges situations that years of two-force analysis had frozen solid.

This week’s assignment is exactly that. Choose one deadlock — one, and choose a real one, something with years on it. Write the three-force analysis in plain sentences: what pushes, what resists (named respectfully, as structure, not sin), and what third force is absent. Then engineer one candidate third force — an appointment, an environmental change, a person, a reframe, a practice of observing the deadlock itself in the moment it operates — and place it, and watch what happens to the relationship between the forces, not just to the outcome. You are learning to see triads. It is, Gurdjieff insisted, learning to see how anything actually happens.

Worked Examples: Six Triads

Before we go on, let’s make this concrete. Here are six ordinary events, each read through the Law of Three. In every case, notice the same anatomy: an active force pushing, a passive force resisting or receiving, and a third force — usually the invisible one — in whose presence the other two can finally combine. Cover the last column and try to name the third force yourself before reading it.

| Event | Active force | Passive force | Neutralizing (third) force | |:------------------|:-----------------------|:-----------------------|:---------------------------| | A seed becomes a plant | Sunlight and water, driving growth | The seed itself — dormant, inert, resisting change | The soil: the medium that holds moisture to the root and warmth to the germ, letting energy and seed combine | | Bread rises | The yeast, actively fermenting | The dough — heavy, dense, going nowhere on its own | The warmth of the proofing place, which brings yeast and dough into working relation | | An argument gets resolved | One person pushing for change | The other’s resistance to it | The shared commitment (the marriage, the friendship, the project) for whose sake both sides bend | | A sale closes | The seller’s offer and pitch | The buyer’s hesitation and inertia | The buyer’s actual need, which relates the offer to the hesitation and tips it | | A student learns to drive | The instructor’s guidance | The student’s fear and untrained habits | The empty parking lot — safe conditions in which instruction can meet fear without disaster | | A new habit takes hold | Your intention | The machine’s inertia | The technique: the alarm clock, the calendar entry, the partner — structure that relates intention to inertia (this whole book is a study of this row) |

Six events, one law. And notice what the table trains you to do: when something in your life is stuck, stop pushing harder (more first force) and stop blaming the resistance (second force is just doing its job). Ask instead: what is the missing third force, and how do I engineer its arrival?

But the Law of Three answers only half the question of why efforts fail. It explains the deadlocks — the projects that never truly start because two forces lock at the threshold. It does not yet explain the other, stranger fate: the projects that do start (genuinely, with all three forces present, moving beautifully) and then, weeks or months in, bend. Drift. They become, by imperceptible degrees, something other than what they set out to be, until the diet has become a cheat-day scheduling system, the startup has pivoted into its own opposite, and the meditation practice has become an app streak. Why does everything that begins straight end crooked?

For that, the tradition has a second law (the Law of Octaves) and it is the next chapter.
